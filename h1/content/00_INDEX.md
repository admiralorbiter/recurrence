You do **not** need a background in machine learning, statistics, cognitive science, or philosophy of mind to follow this site.

Horizon 1 begins with a simple engineering move: give a language model a past.

Not a metaphorical past. A concrete one:

- an event log;
- a working-memory dictionary;
- goals with statuses;
- a source ledger;
- a logical clock;
- a state object that can be serialized, reset, cloned, replayed, and surgically edited.

That sounds like a memory system. It also sounds, if one is not careful, like the beginning of a continuing self.

The entire purpose of H1 is to slow down before making that leap.

> **We gave the machine a notebook, a diary, a dashboard, time to reflect, and a field labeled “Self.” Then we kept asking which of those things actually controlled what happened next.**

## The adventure in seven turns

<div><span>04</span><strong>Reflection</strong><p>Quiet explicit rewriting produces persistent symbolic clutter rather than reliable consolidation.</p></div>

<div><span>07</span><strong>Metacognition</strong><p>Self-framing does not reveal a resolved positive advantage over a matched observer with the same public evidence.</p></div>

## The result in one paragraph

Level-1 scaffolded persistence is useful. It can make history continuously available, compress a growing transcript into a bounded state, support clean experimental interventions, and give the project a reproducible state machine. But the strongest H1 evidence does not make that explicit state private, authoritative, or irreducibly continuous. The raw episodic record remains a powerful competitor. Deterministic state can be replayed. Model-written state is fragile. Quiet reflection can self-pollute. When history and state conflict, changing history changes behavior far more than changing state. And public self-framing does not outperform a strong observer at predicting the correctness of the same decision.

That is not a failed search for a self.

It is a successful specification of what a stronger claim must survive next.

## How to use this site

There are three levels of reading:

1. **The story:** follow the chapters in order.
2. **The evidence:** open the tables, interactives, and source trails.
3. **The audit:** study the Measurement Archaeology and Data Atlas to see how the ruler changed.

Every chapter ends by answering the same questions:

- What did we think might happen?
- What did we actually build?
- What happened in the data?
- What broke or had to be hardened?
- What survived?
- Why did that force the next sprint?
