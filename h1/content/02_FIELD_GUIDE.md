# Field Guide
## How to read the evidence without losing the story

H1 contains thousands of model calls, many conditions, and a great deal of statistical machinery. You do not need to memorize it. You do need to know what each layer is allowed to say.

## Four layers of a scientific statement

<div class="four-layers">
<div><span>1</span><strong>Observation</strong><p>“The condition scored 60.4%.”</p></div>
<div><span>2</span><strong>Effect estimate</strong><p>“It was 20.8 percentage points above another condition.”</p></div>
<div><span>3</span><strong>Inferential decision</strong><p>“The prespecified episode-level test resolved a difference.”</p></div>
<div><span>4</span><strong>Mechanistic interpretation</strong><p>“The difference was caused by reconstruction loss rather than a schema bug.”</p></div>
</div>

A common failure is to leap from layer 1 to layer 4.

H1 repeatedly caught itself doing exactly that.

## Micro accuracy and macro accuracy

**Micro accuracy** gives every trial equal weight.

**Macro accuracy** gives each task family equal weight, even if one family has more items.

In S04, for example, there were 18 delayed-KV probes, 18 source probes, and only 6 goal probes. Micro accuracy therefore weights KV and source more heavily. Macro accuracy prevents the smaller goal family from disappearing inside the average.

## Paired experiments

Many H1 experiments show the **same underlying episode** under multiple conditions. That is stronger than comparing unrelated samples.

A paired contrast asks:

> On this same information problem, what changed when we changed only the representation, timing, or intervention?

## Why the episode is often the unit of uncertainty

Multiple probes from one episode share the same underlying events. Treating every probe as independent would overstate the amount of evidence.

H1 therefore often uses:

- **episode-clustered bootstrap intervals** for effect-size uncertainty;
- **episode-level sign-flip or block-swap tests** for the primary inferential decision;
- **McNemar tests** as supplementary trial-level evidence.

## Confidence intervals and p-values

A 95% confidence interval is not a 95% probability that the true value lies inside the observed interval. It is the interval generated by a procedure designed to cover the target at a stated long-run rate under its assumptions.

A p-value is not the probability the null is true. It measures how surprising a statistic is under a specified null/randomization procedure.

The null matters. In S09, a naive 20% chance test was not enough because the model had a strong “Self” response bias. The corrected permutation preserved the model's actual answers and shuffled source ownership within each episode.

## No resolved difference is not equivalence

When H1 says **no resolved difference**, it means the prespecified test did not distinguish the conditions.

It does not prove:

- the effect is exactly zero;
- the conditions are equivalent;
- the mechanism is identical.

Formal equivalence would require a prespecified smallest effect worth caring about and a test designed around that margin.

## Accuracy is not always the right endpoint

H1 increasingly supplements final-answer accuracy with mechanism-level audit measures:

- exact state hashes;
- prompt hashes;
- omission/mutation partitions;
- phantom-key counts;
- protected-evidence mutation;
- derived-inference precision;
- source confusion matrices;
- state versus memory allegiance;
- Brier score and AUROC.

That is why S07's strongest result is not merely a downstream accuracy difference. It is the audit: 274 derived writes, zero exact correct target derivations.

## The claim ceiling

The strongest H1 evidence concerns explicit prompt-level state and behavior.

It does not settle:

- phenomenal consciousness;
- subjective experience;
- personhood;
- universal claims about all language models;
- whether hidden recurrence will succeed.

A careful conclusion can still be important without being metaphysical.
