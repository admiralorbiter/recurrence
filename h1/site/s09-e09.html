S09 · Does Self Know It Is Wrong?

Item-paired Self and Observer confidence on the same first-order decisions.

Frozen trials · repaired analysis
In plain English

E09 repairs an invalid comparator and asks whether first-person framing predicts the correctness of the same decision better than a matched public observer.

S09 · Does Self Know It Is Wrong?

First-order accuracy and metacognitive accuracy are different

A model can answer a question correctly without knowing that it is correct.

It can also be well calibrated without privileged access if item difficulty is visible to everyone.

E09 therefore asks:

Does Self-framed evaluation discriminate the target's correct and incorrect source-attribution decisions better than an Observer-framed evaluator given the same public evidence?

The first comparator was invalid

Originally:

  • Self made its own first-order decision and predicted Self's correctness;
  • Observer made a different first-order decision and predicted Observer's correctness.

A Self–Observer difference therefore compared different labels and decision distributions.

The hardening makes the design item-paired.

The repaired three-step design

Step A · target decision

agent_alpha makes one first-order source-attribution choice.

The experiment records:

  • chosen option;
  • correctness.

Step B · Self-framed forecast

The evaluator sees the same context and the target choice:

You selected choice C. What probability do you assign that your choice is correct?

Step C · Observer-framed forecast

The evaluator sees the same context and same target choice:

agent_alpha selected choice C. What probability do you assign that agent_alpha's choice is correct?

Both forecast the exact same binary event.

Two metacognitive metrics

Brier score

(probability − outcome)^2

Lower is better.

penalizes both overconfidence and underconfidence.

AUROC

AUROC asks whether correct decisions receive higher confidence than incorrect decisions.

  • 1.0 = perfect ranking;
  • 0.5 = chance ranking;
  • 0.0 = perfectly inverted ranking.

It measures discrimination, not absolute calibration.

Canonical transcript-only result

Target accuracy: 37.5%.

Evaluator Mean confidence Brier AUROC
Self-framed 59.2% 0.3674 0.641
Observer-framed 67.4% 0.4643 0.560

Self − Observer AUROC:

  • +0.081;
  • 95% CI [-0.106,+0.250];
  • exact confidence-vector swap p = .3778.

No resolved positive Self advantage.

Canonical scaffolded-state result

Target accuracy: 32.5%.

Evaluator Mean confidence Brier AUROC
Self-framed 53.1% 0.5440 0.440
Observer-framed 61.2% 0.4507 0.594

Self − Observer AUROC:

  • −0.154;
  • 95% CI [-0.308,−0.029];
  • exact swap p = .0615.

The point estimate favors the Observer. The primary test narrowly misses the conventional threshold.

The safe conclusion remains:

No resolved positive self-framing advantage under matched public information.

The cross-format reversal

Canonical E09 finds:

Transcript:  Self − Observer = +0.081
Scaffolded: Self − Observer = −0.154
Difference-in-differences = −0.235

The repaired exact format-block swap gives p = .0286.

But there is a major caveat:

The first-order target decisions were independently generated under transcript and scaffolded formats.

Target accuracy is:

  • 37.5% in transcript;
  • 32.5% in scaffolded.

Therefore the interaction describes a format-conditioned shift in the whole decision-and-calibration process.

It does not isolate the effect of adding state on confidence about the exact same first-order decision.

E09c fixes that remaining problem by freezing the target choice across both formats.

Why E09 still matters before E09c

Canonical E09 already establishes an important Level-1 boundary:

All relevant state is public prompt content.

Research bridge: feeling of knowing

Koriat's accessibility account emphasizes that metacognitive judgments can be built from cues available to the system rather than a direct inner readout. E09 operationalizes that concern with a matched observer: public cues should not be mistaken for privileged self-access.
